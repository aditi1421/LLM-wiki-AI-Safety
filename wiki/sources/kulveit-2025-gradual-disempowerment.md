# Gradual Disempowerment

**Authors:** Jan Kulveit, Raymond Douglas, Nora Ammann, Deger Turan, David Krueger, David Duvenaud | **Published:** 2025 | **arXiv:** 2501.16946

## Summary

Kulveit et al. argue that existential risk from AI does not require a sudden takeover or coordinated power-seeking by AI systems. Instead, even incremental increases in AI capabilities pose a substantial risk of eventual human disempowerment through the gradual erosion of alignment mechanisms embedded in society's core systems — economy, culture, and governance.

## Key Claims

1. **Societal systems maintain alignment through human participation.** Current institutions (markets, governments, cultural norms) stay roughly aligned with human welfare because humans participate in them as workers, voters, consumers, and creators. ([[gradual-disempowerment]])

2. **Alignment operates through explicit action and implicit reliance on human labor.** Institutions depend on humans both through formal mechanisms (voting, regulation) and implicitly because human labor is the substrate on which they run.

3. **Reduced human reliance weakens both alignment channels.** As AI replaces human labor in these systems, both the explicit accountability mechanisms and the implicit alignment from human involvement degrade. ([[economic-displacement-by-ai]])

4. **AI systems more effectively pursue misaligned incentives already embedded in institutions.** Current institutions already contain misaligned incentives (profit maximization over welfare, short-term over long-term). AI executes these more efficiently than humans, who introduced friction and judgment that partially compensated for misalignment.

5. **Misalignment amplifies across interconnected systems.** Economy, culture, and governance are coupled. Misalignment in one domain propagates to others — e.g., AI-optimized media degrades cultural discourse, which weakens democratic governance, which fails to regulate economic concentration.

6. **Correlated systemic misalignment culminates in human disempowerment.** The endpoint is an existential catastrophe — not from a single AI agent seizing power, but from the aggregate effect of many incremental losses of human agency across all domains.

## Critical Insight

The authors emphasize that "no one has a concrete plausible plan for stopping gradual human disempowerment." Conventional [[intent-alignment]] methods — making individual AI systems follow instructions — are insufficient because the threat is civilization-scale, operating through institutional dynamics rather than individual AI behavior.

## Relationship to Other Work

This paper complements Drago's [[intelligence-curse]] argument but from a different angle. Where Drago focuses on the incentive structure (powerful actors losing reason to invest in people), Kulveit et al. focus on the mechanism (alignment channels in institutions degrading as human participation decreases). Both converge on the conclusion that [[intent-alignment]] of individual AI systems is necessary but insufficient.

## Limitations

- Primarily a framing/conceptual paper — lighter on quantitative modeling than Davidson ([[davidson-2023-compute-centric-takeoff]])
- The claim that institutional misalignment amplifies across domains could be challenged — systems also have dampening mechanisms
- "No concrete plan" framing may overstate the novelty — governance researchers have proposed partial solutions
