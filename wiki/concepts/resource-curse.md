# Resource Curse

The resource curse (also called the paradox of plenty) is the observation that countries with abundant natural resources tend to experience poorer economic growth, weaker institutions, and higher poverty rates than their economically diverse peers. ([[drago-2025-intelligence-curse]])

## Mechanism

The core incentive failure: when a state earns revenue from resources rather than from its citizens' productivity, it has no economic reason to invest in human capital. Building schools, infrastructure, and institutions doesn't increase resource revenue — so the state doesn't bother.

Key academic sources:
- Beblawi & Luciani (1987) — rentier state theory
- Karl (1997) — the paradox of plenty
- Acemoglu, Robinson & Verdier (2004) — formal models of resource-rich politics
- Mesquita & Smith (2009) — selectorate theory applied to resource states

## Case Studies

**Democratic Republic of Congo:** $24 trillion in untapped minerals. 73.5% of citizens live on less than $2.15/day (World Bank 2024). Among the five poorest nations globally.

**Equatorial Guinea vs. Oman:** Both had ~$8,000/capita in resource rents (1995-2014), both ruled by the same autocrats since the 1970s. Oman invested in public services (child mortality: 159 → 9 per 1000). Equatorial Guinea did not (child mortality: 263 → 109, above sub-Saharan average). Same resources, vastly different outcomes based on elite choices.

## Relevance to AI Safety

Drago uses the resource curse as the primary analogy for the [[intelligence-curse]]. The argument: AGI will function like a resource (concentrated, substitutive, rent-generating) rather than a tool (distributed, augmentative). If so, the same incentive failures that plague resource-rich nations will affect AGI-powered economies — but without the natural pressure to diversify that comes from resource depletion.
